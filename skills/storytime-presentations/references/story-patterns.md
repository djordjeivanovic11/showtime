# Story shapes and how to find the world

These are structures for explanation, not scripts to fill with invented drama.
Start from the audience's question and the real evidence. Keep contrary
results and uncertainty when they matter.

## First, find the four load-bearing pieces

Before choosing a shape, find these in the material. Write each in one line.

1. **The unit to follow.** What countable thing moves through the mechanism?
   A token, a bus, an order, a patient sample, a dollar, a request, a vote.
   This becomes the character or the object hero.
2. **The scaffold.** What single picture of the mechanism can stay on screen
   while you explain it? A route, a pipeline, a probability bar list, an
   organ, a machine, a ledger, a map. It must be able to change one part at a
   time.
3. **The governing metaphor.** What place or object *is* this idea, literally
   drawable? A fork in a forest for uncertainty; a leaky pipe for drop-off; a
   gate for a threshold; a ratchet for a feedback loop. Each element of the
   picture must map to one element of the argument. Reject metaphors that only
   work as words.
4. **The bookend.** Which opening image can return at the end, visibly changed
   by what the talk established? (The same bus stop, now with an even
   timetable; the same forest fork, now at dawn.)

If you cannot find a unit and a scaffold, the talk is probably a list of
claims. Find the one mechanism that connects them, or use a **comparison**
shape with a shared scaffold.

## Audience moments that work

- **Higher or lower?** Show a baseline; hide the answer behind a card; ask.
- **Pick a door.** Two or three options; the room votes; reveal the outcome.
- **Spot the problem.** Show the system running; ask where it breaks.
- **Predict the line.** Draw the axes; ask the room to sketch the curve in
  the air; reveal.
- **Count with me.** A grid of units; the room counts the lit ones.

In leadership rooms, keep it dignified: "Before I show you the number, what
would you have guessed?"

## The shapes

### A puzzle explained (learners, big stage)

Open on an observation that creates a fair question ("Why do buses come in
bunches?"). Show the audience's naive model; reveal the missing mechanism one
part at a time on the same scaffold; return to the opening observation, now
explained. End with a short rule and its limits.

### An evidence trail (experts, research)

State the claim and what evidence would distinguish it from alternatives.
Follow the real sequence: measurement, controls, failures, revised inference.
Separate *observed*, *inferred*, and *unresolved* visually (stamps, colour plus
pattern). End with what the evidence now supports and the next decisive test.
A null or inconclusive result can be the ending; say so plainly.

### Answer first (leadership, decisions)

Situation (one beat), complication (one beat), **the answer and the ask** by
the first minute. Then the mechanism that makes the answer true, the options
under the same criteria, the trade-off, and the specific decision requested.
Keep method and sensitivity checks as backup states after the ending. The
scaffold is often a system map the board can point at.

### A case that changes the system (business, product, service, safety)

Follow one representative case through the process. Show where it gets stuck;
zoom out to show whether the case is typical (with a denominator); return to
the same case after the intervention. Never present a fictional composite as
a real person, or one anecdote as a rate.

### A comparison across time or scenarios (strategy, history, forecasts)

Hold one scaffold and scale fixed while one condition changes. Call out what
was held constant and what remains uncertain. Side-by-side is often clearer
than a dramatic transition when exact differences matter.

### A journey with a turn (keynotes, fundraising, all-hands)

A person or object sets out; a turn reveals the hidden mechanism; the stakes
become personal; the ending shows the world after the change. Earn every
emotional beat with a true detail. One number, not ten.

## Test the shape

After outlining, check that the audience could answer three questions
without the slides: *What was the problem? What changed our view? What follows?*
If one answer is missing, repair the sequence before drawing.

## Time and click budget

- Speaking rate: 125–145 words per minute as an estimate (130 default);
  calibrate from a rehearsal when available.
- Per state: `seconds ≈ words ÷ wpm × 60 + 1.5` (the pause for a click).
- Reserve a 5–10% buffer. Exclude Q&A.
- Count clicks, not slides: a 10-minute learner talk is often 50–80 states;
  a 12-minute board talk is often 20–35.
- If over time, cut content. Never fix timing by speaking faster.
- Mark a short route: states that can be skipped without breaking the story.

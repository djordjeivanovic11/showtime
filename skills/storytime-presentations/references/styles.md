# Seven visual storytelling worlds

Use these as art directions, not prebuilt themes. Each must explain a process or
claim through changes in visible objects. The [style lab](../style-lab/README.md)
puts the same story, numbers, and narration in each world so the comparison is
fair. Its HTML demos are visual references, not engine-ready templates. A brand
system or user preference can override the suggestions.

## Original: Pixel Quest

**Good for:** lively research talks, gaming or maker communities, and lessons where
a recurring character can guide the audience through an abstract mechanism.

- **World:** one original pixel-art universe with recurring locations and an
  optional guide character. Reuse a mechanism scaffold; make progress, setbacks,
  and revisits visible in the same place.
- **Graphic language:** integer-scale sprites, limited palette, large pixel-font
  tags, countable grids, and clear labels for measurements versus schematics.
- **Motion:** presenter-controlled builds, character reactions, and deliberate
  scene changes. Keep a readable static route.
- **Voice:** adventurous without losing scientific or business precision.
- **Risk:** many small click states can exhaust time; pixel fonts and dense plots
  can be hard to read. Preserve exact labels and use a conventional readable font
  where precision needs it.

The six directions below are alternatives to this original look. They should
be just as explanatory, not merely less playful palettes.

## 1. Paper Diorama

[View five states](../style-lab/candidates/paper-diorama/renders/contact.png) ·
[Open the offline demo](../style-lab/candidates/paper-diorama/index.html)

**Good for:** classrooms, public science, community talks, onboarding, and subjects
where a person or object can move through the mechanism.

- **World:** a coherent illustrated place (mailroom, workshop, city, ecosystem).
  An original character or object carries the audience's question through it.
- **Graphic language:** purposeful props, readable signs, spatial cause and effect,
  exact-count grids when numbers matter. A return visit to the same scene shows
  what changed.
- **Motion:** controlled travel, one revealed obstacle or consequence at a time.
  Keep a static route with the same facts.
- **Voice:** warm and curious; let the presenter narrate the technical meaning.
- **Risk:** cartoon detail can trivialize a serious subject. Use restrained drawing,
  fewer characters, or a different grammar when credibility would suffer.

## 2. Editorial Ink

[View five states](../style-lab/candidates/editorial-ink/renders/contact.png) ·
[Open the offline demo](../style-lab/candidates/editorial-ink/index.html)

**Good for:** policy, history, investigative work, nonprofits, and evidence-heavy
stories with human consequences.

- **World:** a case file, archive, field notebook, annotated map, or newspaper desk.
- **Graphic language:** large editorial type, source excerpts, maps, marginalia,
  photographic or drawn evidence, and a limited ink-and-paper palette. Show what
  was known at each stage; make uncertainty visible.
- **Motion:** a document opens, a date is highlighted, a source is placed beside
  another. Avoid fake aging or effects that imply authenticity.
- **Voice:** measured; precise captions can carry more text than a keynote slide.
- **Risk:** decorative "documents" can look like genuine evidence. Label
  illustrations and synthetic examples, and cite real artifacts clearly.

## 3. Signal Line

[View five states](../style-lab/candidates/signal-line/renders/contact.png) ·
[Open the offline demo](../style-lab/candidates/signal-line/index.html)

**Good for:** boardrooms, client work, operations, product strategy, and talks that
must lead to a decision.

- **World:** a service system, route map, control room, or architectural plan.
- **Graphic language:** a stable process diagram, clear paths and bottlenecks,
  exact quantities at each gate, a visible decision or trade-off. Use a restrained
  palette and brand typography.
- **Motion:** reveal upstream-to-downstream, then show the counterfactual or
  proposed intervention at the same scale.
- **Voice:** concise: observation, consequence, recommendation.
- **Risk:** generic boxes and arrows flatten the story. Give the process a
  distinctive spatial metaphor and a concrete unit the audience can track.

## 4. Blueprint Atlas

[View five states](../style-lab/candidates/blueprint-atlas/renders/contact.png) ·
[Open the offline demo](../style-lab/candidates/blueprint-atlas/index.html)

**Good for:** research defenses, engineering reviews, medical or scientific
education, and skeptical technical audiences.

- **World:** an atlas, instrument bench, observable system, or series of related
  specimens. Use semantic zoom from full system to a single case and back.
- **Graphic language:** faithful plots, exact axes/units/denominators, labels for
  model versus measurement, uncertainty, matched controls, and traceable sources.
- **Motion:** first show the system, then the measurement, then the comparison.
  Keep critical plots readable as still frames.
- **Voice:** plain-language picture first, technical mechanism second.
- **Risk:** visual density can outrun the speaker. Split mechanism and result into
  adjacent states; never hide uncertainty solely in a note.

## 5. Living Proof

[View five states](../style-lab/candidates/math-workbench/renders/contact.png) ·
[Open the offline demo](../style-lab/candidates/math-workbench/index.html)

**Good for:** mathematics and computer-science classes, quantitative research,
and technical explanations where the audience needs to see *why* a formula is
true before using it.

- **World:** a warm drafting table with a persistent physical mechanism beside
  a mathematical representation of that mechanism. An original researcher
  reacts while the audience watches both views change together.
- **Graphic language:** a quantity has one meaning across a route, a gauge, and
  a geometric area. Hand-built axes, real units, restrained ink colors, and a
  final equation emerge from the picture. This is an original art direction,
  not an imitation of a known math creator's branding or visual identity.
- **Motion:** transform one state variable across every representation. In the
  demo, bus position, gap arcs, gauge split, triangle widths and heights, and
  labels all follow the same gap length.
- **Voice:** intuitive picture first, derivation second, consequence last.
- **Risk:** a diagram can look self-evident to its author while hiding a key
  assumption. Explain why the shaded area represents mean wait; include a
  static path and check tiny near-zero geometry from the back row. The HTML
  prototype has not been verified as a PowerPoint animation.

## 6. Cinema

[View five states](../style-lab/candidates/cinema/renders/contact.png) ·
[Open the offline demo](../style-lab/candidates/cinema/index.html)

**Good for:** large-room keynotes, launches, conference talks, and a small number
of strong ideas that benefit from theatrical scale.

- **World:** a physical data sculpture on a stage: gates, beads, light, layers,
  or other countable objects. The same apparatus returns as the story evolves.
- **Graphic language:** one huge claim or number, generous space, high contrast,
  restrained color accents, and exact countable units where a quantity is shown.
- **Motion:** animate causal change, not decoration. Advance is presenter
  controlled; a static export must preserve each meaningful state.
- **Voice:** few words on screen, fuller explanation in spoken notes.
- **Risk:** spectacle can obscure labels or collapse denominators. Show the
  comparison basis and source legibly; verify actual playback.

## Select or combine

Ask what setting and audience the style must serve. A corporate talk may use
Signal Line for method, Blueprint Atlas for evidence, Living Proof for a
derivation, and a quiet Cinema-sized
moment for the conclusion, provided typography and color stay
coherent. A serious talk can still be visually vivid. Playfulness and seriousness
are choices about tone, not excuses to weaken explanation.

For a fair style test, hold one sample claim, its numbers, screen size, narration,
and evidence label fixed. Give each version five seconds to communicate the gist,
then ask a viewer what changed and why it matters. Compare:

1. Can a new viewer state the intended takeaway?
2. Are the direction of causation and denominators correct?
3. Can text and encoded quantities be read from the expected viewing distance?
4. Does the style fit the audience and the source material?
5. Does the static/exported file preserve the essential meaning?

Do not choose from one beautiful hero frame alone. Try a second, more difficult
slide with real labels or a chart before committing to a full deck.
